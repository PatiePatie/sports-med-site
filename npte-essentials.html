NPTE Essentials: Beyond the Musculoskeletal Core

Mini-textbook depth on the four non-MSK areas the NPTE tests heavily: Neurology, Cardiopulmonary, Integumentary, and Safety, Ethics & Professional Responsibilities. Rule-based, memorizable, exam-shaped.

NPTE Essentials · Module 01

Neurology

This module covers the neurology content area of the NPTE, including motor systems, stroke, TBI, spinal cord injury, neurodegenerative disease, vestibular rehab, peripheral nerve, motor learning, spasticity, and outcome measures. Each topic is presented at NPTE-exam depth with the normal values, red flags, and clinical reasoning the exam demands.

Content is for educational and exam preparation purposes only and is not a substitute for clinical judgment or current evidence-based guidelines.

1.1 Key Neuroanatomy: UMN vs LMN & Major Tracts

The upper motor neuron (UMN) system originates in the cortex and brainstem and descends to synapse on lower motor neurons (LMNs) in the spinal cord or cranial nerve nuclei. The LMN then innervates skeletal muscle. Accurately distinguishing UMN from LMN lesions is one of the highest-yield topics on the NPTE.

ReflexesHyperreflexia; Babinski sign upgoingHyporeflexia or areflexia; no Babinski

FasciculationsAbsentPresent — hallmark of LMN

UMN vs LMN Signs at a Glance

KEYMajor Ascending and Descending Tracts

  • Corticospinal tract (lateral): the primary voluntary motor pathway; decussates at the medullary pyramids. A lesion above the decussation causes CONTRALATERAL deficits; below, IPSILATERAL.
  • Spinothalamic tract: carries pain, temperature, and crude touch; decussates in the anterior white commissure within 1-2 spinal segments of entry. Lesions cause CONTRALATERAL loss of pain/temperature.
  • Dorsal columns (fasciculus gracilis & cuneatus): carry vibration, proprioception, and fine/discriminative touch; decussate in the medulla (internal arcuate fibers). Ipsilateral loss below the lesion.
  • Spinocerebellar tracts: carry unconscious proprioception to the cerebellum; essential for coordination and balance.
  • Corticobulbar tract: innervates cranial nerve motor nuclei; most nuclei receive BILATERAL input except CN VII lower face (contralateral only), CN XII (contralateral), and CN IV (contralateral).

✅Exam Pearl: Tract Localization

A patient with right-sided loss of pain/temperature from the chest down but intact vibration and proprioception on the right suggests a LEFT spinothalamic lesion (e.g., syringomyelia at that level). Always ask: which tract, which side, which level?

1.2 Stroke (CVA): Classification, Territories & Rehabilitation

Stroke is a leading cause of acquired disability in adults. Ischemic stroke (~85% of cases) results from thrombotic or embolic arterial occlusion; hemorrhagic stroke (~15%) results from rupture of a vessel. Onset is sudden — think stroke when a patient has acute unilateral weakness, speech difficulty, or visual field loss.

KEYArtery Territory Deficits

ArteryKey DeficitsPT Implications
ACA (anterior cerebral)Contralateral leg greater than arm weakness; personality/executive changes; urinary incontinenceFocus on LE strengthening, ambulation, cognitive-facilitated mobility
MCA (middle cerebral)Contralateral arm/face greater than leg weakness; aphasia (dominant) or neglect (non-dominant); homonymous hemianopiaMost common stroke; upper extremity rehab, neglect training, ADL retraining
PCA (posterior cerebral)Homonymous hemianopia; visual agnosia; memory deficits (if thalamus involved)Visual scanning training, environmental modification for hemianopia

Major Stroke Syndromes by Artery

KEYBrunnstrom Stages of Motor Recovery

  • Stage I: Flaccidity, no voluntary movement. (Acute phase.)
  • Stage II: Spasticity appears; synergistic movements begin; minimal voluntary control.
  • Stage III: Spasticity peaks; voluntary movement within synergy patterns; little to no isolated movement.
  • Stage IV: Spasticity begins to decline; movements beginning to break out of synergy; some isolated joint control.
  • Stage V: Synergies largely gone; complex movement patterns; near-normal coordination but may be slow.
  • Stage VI: Spasticity absent; isolated, coordinated, voluntary movement; near-normal motor function.

⛔Red Flag: Painful Hemiplegic Shoulder

Shoulder subluxation and impingement are common after stroke. NEVER lift or pull the affected arm. Use a hemisling during transfers, support the arm in bed with pillows, and avoid overhead reaching. Shoulder pain severely limits rehabilitation participation.

Post-stroke rehabilitation begins as soon as the patient is medically stable. Early mobilization (within 24-48 hours) reduces complications. BP precautions: in the acute phase, avoid orthostatic hypotension by progressing position changes slowly; if the patient is on anticoagulants, use caution with modalities that increase bleeding risk (e.g., electrical stimulation over anticoagulated areas). Fall risk is elevated — use proper guarding, gait belt, and non-skid footwear.

KEYCommon Outcome Measures

  • FIM (Functional Independence Measure) or WeeFIM: rates 18 items from 1 (total assist) to 7 (complete independence).
  • Barthel Index: 0-100 scale for ADL; 60 or above suggests functional independence.
  • 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT): safe ambulation speed cutoff for community walking is approximately 0.8 m/s.
  • Timed Up and Go (TUG): greater than 13.5 seconds indicates increased fall risk.
  • Modified Ashworth Scale: 0-4 for spasticity grading (0 = no increase; 4 = rigid).

✅Exam Pearl: Aphasia vs Dysarthria

Aphasia (a language problem — Wernicke or Broca) comes from damage to the DOMINANT hemisphere (usually left). Dysarthria (a motor speech problem — slurred, slow) can occur with any hemisphere lesion. The NPTE loves to test whether you can tell them apart.

1.3 Traumatic Brain Injury: Rancho Los Amigos & Concussion

TBI is classified as focal (contusion, laceration at impact site) or diffuse (diffuse axonal injury from acceleration/deceleration forces). The Rancho Los Amigos Scale of Cognitive Functioning (Levels I-X) is the gold-standard framework for describing recovery stages and guiding PT intervention.

KEYRancho Los Amigos Levels (I-X)

LevelDescriptionPT Implications
INo response — comaSensory stimulation; positioning to prevent contractures; ROM
II-IVGeneralized response through confusion/localizationStructured stimulation; wake-sleep cycles; simple commands; consistency
V-VIConfused/inappropriate through confused/appropriateSafety awareness; supervised ADL; reality orientation; reduce overstimulation
VIIAutomatic/appropriate — carries out routinesCommunity reintegration prep; complex ADL; community mobility
VIII-XPurposeful/standby assistance through modified independentAdvanced ADL, vocational rehab, driving evaluation, fitness

Rancho Los Amigos Scale — PT Implications

KEYConcussion Management

  • Initial 24-48 hours: relative cognitive and physical rest; avoid screens and heavy exertion.
  • Graduated return-to-activity protocol: light aerobic activity, sport-specific exercise, non-contact drills, contact practice, full return. Each step requires 24 hours symptom-free before progressing.
  • Red flags requiring emergency evaluation: worsening headache, repeated vomiting, seizure, focal neurological deficit, deteriorating consciousness, unusual behavior.
  • Return-to-learn should precede return-to-play; academic accommodations (extra time, reduced workload) may be needed for weeks.

⛔Red Flag: Second Impact Syndrome

If a patient with a recent concussion sustains a second head impact BEFORE fully recovering, catastrophic brain swelling can occur (second impact syndrome). NEVER allow return to contact activity while any post-concussive symptoms remain.

1.4 Spinal Cord Injury: Levels, Syndromes & Autonomic Dysreflexia

SCI classification uses the ASIA Impairment Scale and key sensory/motor points. Functional implications depend on the neurological level of injury and whether the injury is complete or incomplete.

KEYFunctional Implications by Level

  • C1-C4: ventilator-dependent; no voluntary arm movement; head/chin control varies; sip-and-puff or head-array power wheelchair.
  • C5: deltoid and biceps functional; can feed self with adaptive equipment; tenodesis grip not yet functional.
  • C6: wrist extensors functional; tenodesis grip possible; can propel manual wheelchair on flat surfaces.
  • C7-T1: triceps and hand intrinsic muscles functional; can perform most transfers independently; use of lap tray for balance.
  • T2-T6: good trunk control above the umbilicus; can use standard wheelchair with good sitting balance.
  • T7-T12: abdominal muscle control progressing; more functional reaching and bending; community ambulation possible with orthoses (long leg braces) for T12.
  • L1-L5 and S1-S5: varying degrees of lower extremity function; ambulation potential depends on muscle groups preserved.

KEYASIA Impairment Scale

  • A — Complete: no motor or sensory function preserved in sacral segments S4-S5.
  • B — Incomplete: sensory but NOT motor function preserved below the neurological level, including sacral segments.
  • C — Incomplete: motor function preserved below the level, with more than half of key muscles grading less than grade 3.
  • D — Incomplete: motor function preserved below the level, with at least half of key muscles grading grade 3 or above.
  • E — Normal: motor and sensory function normal.

KEYIncomplete Cord Syndromes

  • Central cord syndrome: weakness greater in upper extremities than lower; usually from hyperextension injury in an older adult with cervical stenosis. Preserved sensation may be intact or partial.
  • Brown-Sequard syndrome (hemisection): ipsilateral motor loss and proprioception/vibration loss below lesion; contralateral loss of pain/temperature. Caused by penetrating trauma or lateral disc herniation.
  • Anterior cord syndrome: loss of motor function and pain/temperature below lesion; posterior columns (vibration, proprioception) preserved. Often from anterior spinal artery occlusion.
  • Cauda equina vs conus medullaris: cauda equina (LMN lesion, below L1-L2) causes flaccid paralysis, areflexia, saddle anesthesia; conus medullaris (at T12-L1) mixes UMN and LMN signs. Both are surgical emergencies.

⛔EMERGENCY: Autonomic Dysreflexia (AD)

AD occurs in patients with SCI at T6 or above. Trigger: any noxious stimulus below the lesion (full bladder, kinked catheter, skin irritation, bowel impaction, pressure injury). Signs: pounding headache, flushed/warm skin above lesion, profuse sweating above lesion, nasal congestion, bradycardia, SEVERE HYPERTENSION (systolic can exceed 200 mmHg). This is life-threatening. EMERGENCY RESPONSE: (1) Sit the patient upright immediately, (2) Loosen abdominal binder and clothing, (3) Identify and remove the trigger (check catheter first), (4) Monitor BP — if systolic exceeds 150-160 mmHg, administer prescribed antihypertensive (e.g., nifedipine, nitroglycerin paste), (5) Call emergency medical services if BP does not respond. NEVER leave the patient supine — this can cause stroke.

⚠️Respiratory Considerations in SCI

Injuries at C1-C3 may paralyze the phrenic nerve (C3-C5), causing ventilator dependence. C4-C5 injuries compromise diaphragm function; inspiratory capacity is reduced. Between C5 and T6, intercostal and abdominal muscles are progressively lost, reducing cough effectiveness and increasing pneumonia risk. Incentive spirometry, assisted cough techniques, and ventilator weaning protocols are critical PT interventions.

1.5 Parkinson's Disease: Cardinal Signs, Staging & PT Strategy

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra. The cardinal signs are bradykinesia (the cardinal feature required for diagnosis), rigidity (cogwheel or lead-pipe), resting tremor (4-6 Hz, pill-rolling), and postural instability (typically later in disease).

KEYHoehn and Yahr Staging

  • Stage 1: unilateral symptoms only.
  • Stage 2: bilateral symptoms without impairment of balance.
  • Stage 3: bilateral with postural instability; semi-independent; falls begin.
  • Stage 4: severe disability; still able to stand and walk unassisted but requires significant help.
  • Stage 5: wheelchair-bound or bedridden; complete dependence.

KEYMedication Timing and PT Strategy

  • Levodopa/carbidopa: the gold-standard medication. Takes effect within 30-60 minutes. PT sessions should be scheduled during ON periods (when medication is working) for best participation and safety.
  • Dyskinesias: involuntary movements that occur as a side effect of levodopa (usually during peak-dose). Reduce intensity if dyskinesia impairs balance or safety.
  • Off periods: return of parkinsonian symptoms when medication wears off. Sudden OFF can cause freezing, falls. Always have a chair nearby and avoid rushing during transitions.
  • Amantadine may reduce dyskinesia; dopamine agonists (pramipexole, ropinirole) may cause daytime somnolence — caution with ambulation.

KEYFreezing, Festination & Cueing Strategies

  • Freezing of gait: sudden inability to initiate or continue stepping, especially at doorways, turns, or narrow spaces. Not a problem of weakness — a problem of automatic motor programming.
  • Festination: involuntary acceleration with shortening stride length, often preceding a fall.
  • External cueing: rhythmic auditory cues (metronome, music), visual floor lines, or verbal commands can restart gait by engaging alternative motor pathways.
  • Dual-task training: practice walking while performing a cognitive task to improve automaticity.

⚠️Caution: Orthostatic Hypotension in PD

Orthostatic hypotension (systolic drop of 20 mmHg or more within 3 minutes of standing) is common in PD due to autonomic dysfunction and medication effects. Always check orthostatic vitals before ambulation. Progress bed-to-chair and sit-to-stand activities slowly. Compression stockings and adequate hydration help.

✅Exam Pearl: PD vs Essential Tremor

PD resting tremor decreases with voluntary movement; essential tremor is ACTION tremor (worse during movement) and often involves the head and voice. Essential tremor does NOT respond to levodopa. The NPTE may test this distinction.

1.6 Multiple Sclerosis: Types, Uhthoff's Phenomenon & Rehabilitation

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune demyelinating disease of the central nervous system. It typically presents in young adults (20-40 years), with a female-to-male ratio of approximately 3:1. Lesions are disseminated in time and space (multiple areas, different ages of lesion).

KEYTypes of MS

  • Relapsing-Remitting MS (RRMS): ~85% of initial diagnoses. Discrete attacks (relapses) followed by partial or complete recovery (remission). No progressive worsening between attacks.
  • Primary Progressive MS (PPMS): ~10-15% of cases. Steady worsening from onset without distinct relapses or remissions.
  • Secondary Progressive MS (SPMS): most RRMS patients eventually transition to a progressive phase with gradual worsening, with or without superimposed relapses.

KEYUhthoff's Phenomenon

Uhthoff's phenomenon is a temporary worsening of neurological symptoms when core body temperature rises (even by 0.5 degrees Celsius). Common triggers include hot baths, exercise in hot environments, fever, and humidity. This is NOT a true relapse — it is a conduction block in demyelinated nerves due to temperature sensitivity. Symptoms reverse when the body cools. Exercise should be performed in COOL environments with adequate hydration.

KEYKey PT Considerations in MS

  • Spasticity: common (affects 60-80% of MS patients); managed with stretching, positioning, tone-reducing orthoses, and medications (baclofen, tizanidine).
  • Fatigue: the MOST common and debilitating symptom; energy conservation strategies (pacing, activity-rest scheduling, prioritization), cooling strategies, and exercise (which paradoxically reduces fatigue). Avoid the term beat yoga — use energy conservation or fatigue management.
  • Optic neuritis: painful eye movement with visual loss; common presenting symptom. The patient may have reduced visual acuity — ensure safe ambulation with appropriate assistive devices.
  • Exercise evidence: regular aerobic and resistance exercise is SAFE and BENEFICIAL in MS — reduces fatigue, improves mood, and may slow progression. Avoid overheating.
  • Disease-modifying medications (e.g., interferons, glatiramer, fingolimod): PT should be aware that some require injection-site monitoring, others may cause bradycardia (fingolimod) — monitor vitals.

💡Exam Pearl: MS Relapse vs Uhthoff's

A true MS relapse lasts at least 24 hours, occurs at least 30 days after a previous relapse, and is NOT explained by fever or infection. Uhthoff's phenomenon resolves within hours when the body cools and does NOT count as a relapse. This distinction matters for medication management.

1.7 GBS, ALS & Post-Polio Syndrome

These three conditions share the feature of progressive or subacute motor weakness but differ critically in etiology, trajectory, and management. Misidentifying them on the NPTE can lead to dangerous interventions.

KEYGuillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)

  • Ascending flaccid paralysis: classically starts in the legs and ascends (may reach respiratory muscles).
  • Areflexia or hyporeflexia: the hallmark — distinguishes GBS from UMN conditions.
  • Autonomic instability: blood pressure swings, cardiac arrhythmias, gastroparesis. Monitor vitals closely during PT sessions.
  • Respiratory monitoring: forced vital capacity (FVC) can drop rapidly. A FVC below 20 mL/kg or a rapid decline requires ICU-level monitoring.
  • Recovery: typically begins 2-4 weeks after plateau; may take 6-12 months. IVIG or plasmapheresis can shorten the acute phase.

KEYAmyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS)

  • Both UMN AND LMN signs in the same patient: spasticity + fasciculations + atrophy + hyperreflexia (but fasciculations appear before reflexes disappear in affected segments).
  • Bulbar involvement: dysarthria, dysphagia, tongue fasciculations — indicates poorer prognosis.
  • Overwork weakness: exercise should be submaximal (50-70% of max). Vigorous exercise may ACCELERATE motor neuron degeneration in ALS. This is the OPPOSITE of the general exercise prescription.
  • Life expectancy: typically 3-5 years from diagnosis; ventilatory support decisions must be addressed early.

KEYPost-Polio Syndrome (PPS)

  • New weakness, fatigue, and pain 15-40 years after the original polio infection.
  • Caused by degeneration of surviving motor neurons that have been overworking for decades.
  • Exercise must be carefully dosed: low to moderate intensity, avoid overwork. Supervised progressive resistive exercise is acceptable but fatigue monitoring is essential.

⛔Red Flag: Respiratory Decline in GBS

GBS can progress to respiratory failure within hours to days. Monitor respiratory rate, use of accessory muscles, ability to count to 20 in one breath, and serial FVC measurements. If FVC drops below 20 mL/kg or below 30% of predicted, the patient may need intubation. This is a medical emergency.

⚠️Caution: Exercise Dosing in ALS vs MS

In MS, moderate-to-vigorous exercise is encouraged and evidence-based. In ALS, exercise must be submaximal — the goal is to maintain function without causing overwork weakness. On the NPTE, a question describing a patient with BOTH upper and lower motor neuron signs is describing ALS, NOT MS.

1.8 Vestibular & Balance: BPPV, Central vs Peripheral, & Vestibular Rehab

Vestibular disorders are a major cause of dizziness, imbalance, and falls. The NPTE focuses on the PT's role in assessment and treatment, particularly distinguishing peripheral (benign) from central (dangerous) causes of vertigo.

KEYBPPV (Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo)

  • Most common vestibular disorder; caused by displaced otoliths (calcium carbonate crystals) entering the semicircular canals.
  • Posterior canal is involved in approximately 80-90% of BPPV cases.
  • Dix-Hallpike test: gold standard for posterior canal BPPV. Patient moves from sitting to head-hanging-right with quick rotation. Positive = upbeating torsional nystagmus with latency (2-10 sec) and fatigability.
  • Epley maneuver (canalith repositioning): series of head positions to move otoliths out of the posterior canal back into the utricle. Single treatment resolves BPPV in approximately 80% of cases.
  • Canalithiasis: otoliths free-floating in the canal; cupulolithiasis: otoliths adhered to the cupula; canalithiasis responds better to repositioning maneuvers.

KEYCentral vs Peripheral Nystagmus Clues

FeaturePeripheralCentral
Nystagmus directionAlways horizontal or torsional; never purely verticalMay be purely vertical, purely horizontal, or direction-changing
FatigabilityYes — nystagmus decreases with repeated testingNo — nystagmus persists or worsens
Hearing lossCommon (vestibulocochlear nerve involvement)Uncommon

Peripheral vs Central Vestibular Signs

KEYVestibular Rehab Principles

  • Habituation: repeated exposure to movements that provoke dizziness to reduce symptom severity over time.
  • Gaze stabilization: exercises to keep a visual target clear during head movement (VOR training), improving the vestibulo-ocular reflex
  • Substitution: using other sensory systems (vision, proprioception) to compensate for vestibular loss.
  • Balance and gait training: progressive challenges to static and dynamic balance in safe environments.

KEYKey Balance Outcome Measures

  • Berg Balance Scale: 56-point scale; scores below 45/56 indicate increased fall risk.
  • Timed Up and Go (TUG): greater than 13.5 seconds = increased fall risk; greater than 14 seconds = falls with 100% sensitivity in some studies.
  • Dynamic Gait Index (DGI): 8-item test; scores below 19/24 indicate high fall risk.
  • Mini-BESTest: tests anticipatory, reactive, sensory, and dynamic balance; 28-point scale.
  • 5x Sit-to-Stand: greater than 15 seconds is considered abnormal in most populations.

💡Exam Pearl: Meniere's Disease

Meniere's disease presents with the classic triad: episodic vertigo, unilateral fluctuating hearing loss, and tinnitus with a feeling of aural fullness. It is caused by endolymphatic hydrops (increased pressure in the inner ear). Management includes low-sodium diet, diuretics, and vestibular rehab. On the NPTE, the distinguishing clue from BPPV is the presence of hearing loss.

1.9 Peripheral Nerve: Radiculopathy, Plexopathy & Mononeuropathy

Peripheral nerve disorders present with lower motor neuron signs (weakness, atrophy, hyporeflexia, sensory changes in a specific distribution). The NPTE tests your ability to localize the lesion by pattern of weakness, sensory loss, and reflex change.

KEYRadiculopathy vs Plexopathy vs Mononeuropathy

TypeDistributionKey Clue
RadiculopathyDermatomal (single nerve root territory); weakness + sensory change in same root distributionPain radiates along the dermatome; reflex loss matches the root level (e.g., C6 root = reduced biceps reflex)
PlexopathyMultiple nerve territories from one plexus; mixed pattern of weakness and sensory lossBrachial plexus: Erb or Klumpke patterns; lumbosacral plexus: variable lower extremity pattern

Localization of Peripheral Nerve Lesions

KEYBrachial Plexus Key Patterns

  • Erb-Duchenne (upper trunk, C5-C6): waiter's tip position — arm adducted, internally rotated, forearm pronated, wrist flexed. From birth trauma or motorcycle fall.
  • Klumpke (lower trunk, C8-T1): claw hand (flexed MCP, extended IPs) from intrinsic hand muscle paralysis; if T1 sympathetic fibers involved, ipsilateral Horner syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis).

KEYKey Mononeuropathies

  • Carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve at wrist): weakness of opponens pollicis, abductor pollicis brevis, flexor pollicis brevis; sensory loss in lateral 3.5 digits; thenar atrophy in chronic cases; Phalen and Tinel signs positive.
  • Ulnar nerve at elbow: weakness of interossei, adductor pollicis, flexor digitorum profundus (ring and small fingers); sensory loss on ulnar 1.5 digits; claw hand (more pronounced if lesion is distal to FCU).
  • Radial nerve at spiral groove (Saturday night palsy): wrist drop and finger extension weakness; sensory loss on dorsal hand (first web space); usually from prolonged compression (e.g., sleeping with arm over a chair).
  • Common peroneal (fibular) nerve at fibular head: foot drop (loss of ankle dorsiflexion and eversion); sensory loss on lateral leg and dorsum of foot; steppage gait. Compression from tight casts, prolonged leg crossing, or knee surgery.

✅Exam Pearl: Dermatomes and Myotomes

Key upper extremity landmarks for the NPTE: C5 = deltoid and biceps; C6 = wrist extensors and biceps reflex; C7 = triceps and wrist flexors; C8 = finger flexors and thumb movements; T1 = intrinsic hand muscles. For lower extremity: L2-L3 = hip flexion (iliopsoas); L4 = knee extension (quadriceps, patellar reflex); L5 = ankle dorsiflexion and great toe extension; S1 = ankle plantarflexion and Achilles reflex.

1.10 Motor Control & Motor Learning Principles

Neurological rehabilitation relies on principles of motor learning and motor control to retrain movement after CNS damage. The PT designs interventions based on how the nervous system acquires, refines, and retains motor skills.

KEYFitts and Posner's Three Stages of Motor Learning

  • Cognitive stage: the learner understands the task through verbal cues and demonstration; movement is inconsistent, error-prone, and requires high cognitive demand.
  • Associative stage: movement becomes smoother and more consistent; errors decrease; less cognitive effort is required; the learner begins to refine the skill.
  • Autonomous stage: movement is automatic, efficient, and consistent; the performer can multitask; errors are rare; the skill is largely retained.

KEYFeedback Types

  • Knowledge of Results (KR): information about the OUTCOME of the movement (e.g., 'you cleared the obstacle'). Best for tasks with an external focus.
  • Knowledge of Performance (KP): information about the QUALITY or pattern of the movement (e.g., 'your knee extended fully on that step'). Best for refining technique.
  • Faded feedback: gradually reducing feedback frequency as learning progresses promotes retention. Constant feedback can create dependency.
  • Bandwidth feedback: providing feedback only when performance deviates beyond a set tolerance (e.g., only cue if knee flexion exceeds 30 degrees).

KEYPractice Types and Neuroplasticity

  • Blocked practice: practicing the same skill repeatedly before moving to the next. Better for INITIAL acquisition but poorer retention and transfer.
  • Random practice: interleaving different skills within a session. Slower initial learning but SUPERIOR retention and transfer. More representative of real-world demands.
  • Massed practice: continuous practice with little rest. More efficient for time but increases fatigue. Appropriate when endurance is the goal.
  • Distributed practice: practice sessions spread over time with rest intervals. Better for retention and skill acquisition.
  • Task-specific training: practice should replicate the actual task (e.g., practicing sit-to-stand transfers for a patient who needs to get out of bed).
  • Constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT): restraining the unaffected limb while intensively practicing with the affected limb; evidence-based for chronic stroke to improve UE function.

✅Exam Pearl: Blocked vs Random Practice

The NPTE will describe a patient performing repetitive sit-to-stand 20 times in a row (blocked practice) vs performing sit-to-stand, then reaching, then stepping (random practice). Random practice is harder in the moment but produces BETTER long-term learning and transfer. If the question asks what promotes learning, choose random practice.

1.11 Spasticity Management: Medications, Casting & Toxin

Spasticity is a velocity-dependent increase in tone resulting from upper motor neuron lesions. It is not always pathological — some tone can assist with transfers and positioning. Management is considered when spasticity causes pain, limits ROM, impairs function, or creates hygiene/skin-care challenges.

MedicationMechanismKey Caution
BaclofenGABA-B agonist; inhibits spinal reflexesSedation; respiratory depression at high doses; do NOT stop abruptly (seizure risk)
TizanidineAlpha-2 agonist; reduces tone centrallyMonitor liver function; sedation; hypotension
DantroleneDirectly acts on skeletal muscle; blocks calcium release from sarcoplasmic reticulumHepatotoxicity risk; monitor LFTs; avoid in active liver disease
DiazepamBenzodiazepine; enhances GABA-A; sedation and hypotoniaHighly sedating; dependency risk; respiratory depression; NOT first-line

Oral Antispasticity Medications

KEYBotulinum Toxin (Botox)

  • Mechanism: blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, causing focal chemical denervation.
  • Onset: 3-7 days; peak effect at 2-6 weeks; duration 3-6 months.
  • Most effective when combined with targeted physical therapy (stretching, strengthening, splinting) during the window of reduced tone.
  • Condications: active infection at injection site, pregnancy, aminoglycoside use (potentiates effect).
  • Adverse effect: temporary weakness in the targeted or adjacent muscles.

KEYSerial Casting vs Stretching Evidence

  • Serial casting: progressive stretching by applying a new cast at increasing ROM increments every 5-7 days. Effective for established contracture.
  • Prolonged stretch (static progressive splinting): low-load, long-duration stretch applied continuously for 6-8 hours. Evidence supports effectiveness comparable to serial casting for mild-to-moderate contracture.
  • Stretching evidence: static stretching held 30-60 seconds for multiple repetitions is the standard for tone management. Stretching must be combined with activity-based interventions for functional outcomes.

⛔Red Flag: Heterotopic Ossification

Heterotopic ossification (HO) is abnormal bone formation in soft tissue, most common after TBI, SCI, and hip fracture. Signs include: progressive loss of ROM, warmth, swelling, and erythema around a joint. Prevention: early ROM exercises, position management, and possible indomethacin prophylaxis. Do not force ROM if HO is suspected — obtain imaging first.

⚠️Caution: Baclofen Withdrawal

Abrupt discontinuation of baclofen (especially intrathecal baclofen) can cause life-threatening withdrawal: hyperthermia, severe spasticity rebound, rhabdomyolysis, autonomic instability, and seizures. Always taper baclofen gradually under medical supervision.

1.12 Outcome Measures & Disposition Quick Reference

The NPTE requires you to know common outcome measures, their scoring, and key cutoff scores. This section consolidates the measures referenced throughout this module.

KEYStrength Assessment

  • Manual Muscle Testing (MMT) — Oxford Scale: 0 = no contraction; 1 = trace (flicker); 2 = active movement with gravity eliminated; 3 = active movement against gravity; 3+ = against gravity with some resistance; 4- = moderate resistance; 4 = moderate-to-good resistance; 4+ = good resistance; 5 = normal (full resistance).
  • Handheld dynamometry: produces objective force measurements in kilograms or Newtons; useful for tracking strength changes over time.

KEYFunctional Independence and ADL

  • FIM (Functional Independence Measure): 18 items, each scored 1-7 (total 18-126). Covers self-care, sphincter control, transfers, locomotion, communication, social cognition. 126 = fully independent; below 74 indicates need for extensive assistance.
  • Barthel Index: 10 ADL items, total 0-100. Score of 60 or above = functional independence; below 20 = severe functional dependence.

KEYMobility and Gait

  • 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT): measures comfortable walking speed. Community ambulation threshold: approximately 0.8 m/s; household ambulation: approximately 0.4 m/s.
  • 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT): measures endurance. Normal for healthy older adults: approximately 400-700 meters. Useful for tracking aerobic capacity in neuro populations.
  • Timed Up and Go (TUG): greater than 13.5 seconds = increased fall risk. Dual-task TUG adds cognitive challenge; greater than 20 seconds on dual-task = high risk.

KEYBalance Measures

  • Berg Balance Scale (BBS): 56-point scale; below 45/56 = increased fall risk; below 40/56 = faller classification (high risk). Higher scores = better balance.
  • Dynamic Gait Index (DGI): 8 items, 24 points; below 19/24 = high fall risk.
  • Mini-BESTest: 28 points; tests anticipatory, reactive, sensory, and dynamic balance; lower scores = greater impairment.
  • Functional Reach Test: maximum distance a person can reach forward while standing; less than 6 inches (15 cm) may indicate increased fall risk.

KEYSpasticity, Cognition & Disease-Specific

  • Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS): 0 = no increase; 1 = slight catch; 1+ = catch with minimal resistance; 2 = marked increase through most of range; 3 = considerable increase, difficult to move; 4 = rigid.
  • Rancho Los Amigos Scale: I-X for cognitive function after TBI (see section 1.3).
  • ASIA Impairment Scale: A-E for SCI severity (see section 1.4).
  • Brunnstrom Stages: I-VI for motor recovery post-stroke (see section 1.2).

✅Exam Pearl: Choosing the Right Outcome Measure

The NPTE loves scenario-based questions asking which outcome measure to use. Key rule: match the measure to the domain. Mobility? Use 10MWT or TUG. Balance? Use Berg or DGI. ADL? Use FIM or Barthel. Spasticity? Use Modified Ashworth. Cognition after TBI? Use Rancho. SCI severity? Use ASIA. Motor recovery after stroke? Use Brunnstrom. If the question asks for fall risk prediction, TUG greater than 13.5s and Berg less than 45 are your answers.

NPTE Essentials · Module 02

Cardiopulmonary

Cardiopulmonary content is roughly 15-19% of the NPTE and it also hides inside every system scenario: vital signs, red flags, and exercise response show up in almost every case. Master the numbers, the precautions, and the stop-signs — they are the most frequently tested pieces.

2.1 Exercise Response in Health

With exercise the heart rate rises, stroke volume rises (then plateaus), cardiac output and systolic BP rise, systemic vascular resistance falls, ventilation increases, and the exercising muscle extracts more oxygen. Long-term training shifts the curve: lower resting HR, higher stroke volume, and a lower submaximal HR at the same workload.

KEYKey numbers the NPTE reuses

  • 1 MET = 3.5 ml oxygen per kg per min (resting oxygen consumption); activity levels are graded in METs (e.g. light below 3, moderate 3-6, vigorous above 6).
  • Rate-pressure product (RPP) = HR x systolic BP; it estimates myocardial oxygen demand. Keep RPP controlled with acute exertional angina or high-risk cardiac patients.
  • Karvonen target HR = (max HR - resting HR) x intensity + resting HR; commonly 60-85% of heart-rate reserve.
  • Maximum heart rate estimate = 220 - age.
  • Borg RPE 6-20: 13 = somewhat hard, 15 = hard, 17 = very hard. Borg CR10: 5-6 is usually a vigorous level.
  • Normal BP response: systolic rises roughly 10 mmHg per MET, diastolic stays stable. Failure to rise, or an exercise drop in systolic BP, is a red flag to stop.

2.2 Exercise Testing & Monitoring

Vital-sign monitoring during exercise: HR (radial/apical or telemetry), BP (auscultatory, same arm each time), SpO2 (goal 90-94% general, 88-92% in COPD), and symptoms (dyspnea, chest pain, dizziness). The PT should know when to STOP and re-evaluate.

KEYStop signs for exercise (any ONE is enough)

  • Angina or chest tightness (stop, rest, help the patient sit down, use prescribed nitroglycerin per protocol, get help if not relieved in ~5 min).
  • Severe dyspnea disproportionate to effort, or SpO2 dropping below ~88%.
  • Dizziness, lightheadedness, near-syncope, or feeling faint.
  • Systolic BP that drops or fails to rise with increasing workload.
  • New ECG changes (ST depression/elevation) or significant arrhythmia — on telemetry this is a hard stop.
  • Pallor, cyanosis, confusion, or a sudden change in mental status.
  • Patient asks to stop. Respect it.

KEYContraindications to exercise testing / programmed aerobic exercise

  • Acute myocardial infarction within the first ~2-5 days (per supervising physician), unstable angina at rest or rapid crescendo.
  • Severe aortic stenosis (syncope with exertion is classic).
  • Acute myocarditis or pericarditis, acute pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis, acute aortic dissection.
  • Uncontrolled hypertension (very high resting BP per physician), or resting tachycardia with unknown cause.
  • Fever, acute infection, or within a few hours of a recent high-intensity exercise bout for some tests.
  • The key PT phrase: red flags and absolute contraindications belong to the physician to clear; the PT implements and monitors.

2.3 Coronary Artery Disease & Cardiac Rehab

CAD is the build-up of atherosclerotic plaque in coronary arteries. Stable angina is predictable (same threshold, relieved by rest/nitroglycerin); unstable angina is new, more frequent, or occurs at rest — a medical emergency; variant (Prinzmetal's) angina is coronary spasm. MI occurs when plaque ruptures and occludes a vessel.

  • Classic MI symptoms: substernal crushing chest pain/pressure radiating to left arm, jaw or back, diaphoresis, nausea, shortness of breath.
  • Women and diabetic patients often have atypical symptoms: fatigue, dyspnea, epigastric or back discomfort, no classic chest pain. Do not dismiss new dyspnea or profound fatigue.
  • Unstable angina and MI = emergency: stop activity, call for help per facility protocol, do not leave the patient alone.

KEYAngina protocol & monitoring

  • If angina develops: STOP exercise, sit the patient down, note the time, administer the patient's prescribed nitroglycerin (typically 1 tablet/spray, may repeat every ~5 min per physician order, commonly up to 3 doses), and call for help if pain persists beyond ~5 minutes or is severe.
  • Measure HR and BP during and ~2-5 minutes after exercise; the patient should recover toward baseline in a few minutes.
  • Anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs) raise bleeding risk — use padded/grated surfaces carefully, watch for bruising/bleeding, and communicate lab values (e.g. INR) with the team before aggressive techniques.
  • Post-MI: exercise progression should follow physician clearance; typical early limits include keeping HR and RPP below prescribed ceilings and avoiding Valsalva.

⛔Sternal precautions (post-CABG)

After CABG the sternum takes ~6-8 weeks to heal. Avoid lifting more than 5-10 lb (about 2-5 kg), no pushing/pulling through the arms, no overhead lifting or >90-degree arm elevation, no using arms to push up from chairs/bed, and teach log-roll transfers with a pillow held against the chest when coughing or sneezing.

Heart failure is the inability of the heart to meet tissue demand. KEYFluid overload flags (call the physician)

  • Rapid weight gain more than about 2-3 lb (about 1-1.4 kg) in 24 hours or 5 lb in a week.
  • Increasing leg/ankle edema, new or worsening orthopnea, PND, or sleeping on more pillows.

KEYExercise approach

  • Exercise improves peak VO2, symptoms, and quality of life and is safe in compensated HF: use symptom-limited aerobic (walking, cycling) with RPE and HR guidance, plus low-load resistance.

Beta-blockers (core therapy in HF) blunt the HR response — use RPE and symptom tolerance, not target HR formulas alone, to dose intensity.

2.5 Hypertension

Hypertension is the most common cardiovascular condition encountered in practice. Classification context commonly tested: normal below 120/80, elevated 120-129/below 80, stage 1 at or above 130/80, stage 2 at or above 140/90 (verify current guideline wording).

  • Regular aerobic exercise plus isometric resistance training (e.g. wall squats, handgrip) lowers BP by roughly 5-10 mmHg; medication may be needed additionally.
  • For hypertensives, avoid heavy-load lifting with Valsalva and sudden maximal efforts, which spike BP acutely.
  • Antihypertensives and PT: diuretics (monitor hydration/orthostasis), ACE inhibitors (watch for dry cough, hyperkalemia), ARBs similar, beta-blockers (blunt HR — use RPE), calcium channel blockers (ankle edema, flushing) — many cause orthostatic hypotension in the elderly; screen with lie-to-stand.

⚠️Precaution

Symptoms during transfer: dizziness, lightheadedness, blurred vision — stop the transfer, sit the patient down, rest, and reassess. Never 'push through' orthostatic symptoms.

2.6 Peripheral Arterial Disease

PAD is atherosclerotic narrowing of arteries to the legs. Classic intermittent claudication: reproducible calf cramping pain with a consistent walking distance, relieved by rest within a few minutes. Critical ischemia adds rest pain, cool/pale foot, and tissue loss.

  • ABI (ankle-brachial index): normal about 1.0-1.4; below 0.9 suggests PAD; above 1.4 suggests non-compressible (calcified) vessels, often diabetic.
  • Exercise program: walking to moderate claudication pain, then rest until pain resolves, then repeat — interval walking is the proven approach, typically 30-45 min, 3-5 days/week, several months.
  • Cautions: avoid leg elevation in severe PAD (worsens ischemia), inspect footwear/hygiene, and refer ulceration or rest pain promptly.

⛔Red flag

Sudden pallor, pain, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis in one limb suggest acute limb ischemia (embolus/thrombosis) — emergency referral, do not exercise the limb.

COPD includes emphysema (alveolar destruction, loss of elastic recoil, air trapping) and chronic bronchitis (chronic cough with sputum for at least 3 months in 2 consecutive years, airway inflammation and mucus). Most patients have both.

2.8 Asthma

Asthma is chronic airway inflammation with reversible bronchoconstriction and airway hyperresponsiveness. Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB) typically peaks 5-15 minutes after exercise ends and is worst in cold, dry air.

  • Exercise strategy: 5-15 min warm-up, use prescribed pre-exercise reliever (SABA) if ordered, choose warm humid environments, gradual cool-down, keep reliever inhaler available at the session.
  • Acute attack: stop activity, sit upright, give the patient their rescue inhaler, monitor symptoms/SpO2, and escalate to emergency care if not improving, breathing is labored, or the patient cannot speak in full sentences.
  • Severe-attack warning signs (need urgent care): inability to speak in sentences, use of accessory muscles, silent chest (barely audible wheeze — danger), cyanosis, confusion, or SpO2 below ~90%.

⛔Red flag

A 'silent chest' in an asthma attack is ominous — wheezing that suddenly becomes inaudible can mean almost no air is moving. This is an emergency, not relief.

2.9 Restrictive Lung Disease

Restrictive disorders reduce lung volumes (low TLC, FVC, FEV1 with normal or high FEV1/FVC). Examples: interstitial lung disease/pulmonary fibrosis, neuromuscular weakness (ALS, GBS, SCI high levels), chest-wall restriction (kyphoscoliosis, ankylosing spondylitis), and obesity hypoventilation.

  • IPF: progressive exertional dyspnea and dry cough, bilateral basilar 'Velcro' crackles, clubbing; no obstructive spirometry; exercise is limited by dyspnea.
  • Obesity hypoventilation: daytime somnolence, morning headache (nocturnal CO2 retention), polycythemia/cyanosis; treat with weight loss and sometimes PAP — assess endurance with rest, upright positioning.
  • Neuromuscular weakness: monitor decline in vital capacity; assist cough (quad cough / manual assistance), encourage secretion clearance, position for comfort, and report acute respiratory changes.

💡Pearl

Restrictive = small lungs (low volumes). Obstructive = slow emptying (low FEV1/FVC). Differentiation on spirometry is a staple NPTE topic.

2.10 Pulmonary Embolism & DVT

Venous thromboembolism (DVT + PE) is a leading preventable hospital complication. Virchow's triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Immobility, surgery, cancer, pregnancy, obesity, and oral contraceptives all raise risk.

KEYRecognizing DVT

  • Unilateral calf/thigh swelling, warmth, tenderness, and red or darkened color; a positive Homan sign is NOT reliable — do not rely on it or provoke it.
  • If you suspect DVT: stop the activity, do NOT massage or aggressively move the limb, notify the physician, keep the patient at rest.

KEYRecognizing PE (emergency)

  • Sudden onset shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain (worse with breath), tachycardia, cough, sometimes hemoptysis; severe cases with hypotension, cyanosis, or syncope.
  • Response: stop all activity, sit the patient in a position of comfort, call for emergency help, monitor vitals. Do not return to the session.

⛔Red flag

Any patient who suddenly cannot breathe, has pleuritic chest pain plus tachycardia, or collapses after being immobile for days must be evaluated immediately for PE. Early mobility programs reduce DVT risk — but only when cleared.

Integumentary System

The integumentary system is a small but heavily-weighted niche on the NPTE: staging systems, wound-healing phases, dressing choices, burn calculations, and compression rules are memorizable points that reliably appear. Master the classifications and the red flags.

3.1 Skin Structure & Wound Healing Phases

The skin has three layers: epidermis (outer barrier, renews constantly), dermis (collagen, blood vessels, nerves, glands), and hypodermis/subcutaneous tissue (fat, larger vessels). Injury depth determines healing and treatment.

KEYHealing by intention

  • Primary intention: clean surgical edges approximated — minimal scar (e.g. sutured incision).
  • Secondary intention: wound left open, fills with granulation from the base (e.g. deep pressure ulcer, contaminated wound).
  • Tertiary intention (delayed primary): wound initially left open, then closed, often after infection is controlled (contaminated wounds).

KEYFactors impairing healing

  • Diabetes (microvascular + immune), smoking (vasoconstriction, low oxygen), malnutrition (protein/vitamin C/zinc), infection, ongoing pressure/shear/friction, edema, corticosteroids, advanced age, anemia.
  • Granulation tissue is beefy red and moist — a healthy sign; slough (yellow/white) and necrotic eschar (black) are barriers that must be removed for healing.

💡Pearl

A dry wound cannot heal well, but a saturated dressing is equally harmful. The exam favors the MOISTURE BALANCE principle: moist enough for cellular activity, not so wet that the skin macerates.

3.7 Lymphedema

Lymphedema is chronic tissue swelling from impaired lymphatic drainage. Primary (congenital/inherited) or secondary (post-surgery node dissection — commonly breast cancer, radiation, infection). Characterized by non-pitting to pitting edema, fibrosis, and a positive Stemmer sign (inability to pinch the dorsal foot/toe skin).

KEYStages (contextual)

  • Stage 0: latent, no visible swelling but fluid shifts with position. Stage 1: pitting edema, reduces with elevation. Stage 2: non-pitting with fibrosis, does not reduce with elevation alone. Stage 3: lymphostatic elephantiasis with skin changes.

KEYComplete Decongestive Therapy (CDT)

  • Four components: manual lymph drainage (MLD), short-stretch bandaging and compression garments, therapeutic exercise with compression, and meticulous skin care.
  • Exercise should be performed WITH compression on; relative rest for the limb is not the goal — controlled movement aids flow.
  • Prevent complications: avoid blood draws/needlesticks in the affected limb (post-mastectomy risk), monitor for cellulitis, keep skin moisturized.

⚠️Contraindications / precautions to CDT

Hesitate/refuse aggressive MLD-compression in: active infection or cellulitis, acute DVT or undiagnosed swelling, congestive heart failure or renal failure with fluid overload, severe arterial insufficiency. These are reasons to verify with the physician first.

KEYOutcome measurement

  • Serial limb circumference at marked landmarks (e.g. every 4 cm) and/or water displacement volume. Document consistently at the same landmarks and time of day.

Transmission precautions and PPE

KEYClean vs sterile technique

  • Chronic, non-infected wounds: clean technique is usually acceptable (facility policy governs). Acute surgical wounds, burns, and immunocompromised patients often require sterile technique.
  • Dressing change principle: change when soiled/saturated or per order, remove exudate, never disturb the healing base, and red-flag worsening signs.

KEYSpecial wounds

  • Surgical incision: staples/sutures typically removed around post-op days 7-14 depending on site (per surgeon); monitor for dehiscence and infection.
  • Amputation residual limb: keep the stump protected post-op, start desensitization (tapping, textures) and volume-management (shrinker sock, rigid dressing) as allowed, then prosthetic training.
  • Needlestick: wash immediately with soap and water, report per policy, follow post-exposure prophylaxis guidance.

⚠️Precaution

Sterile or clean? Never guess for a specific patient — facility policy and physician orders determine technique. When in doubt, use the more strict (sterile) approach for wounds you did not create.

3.9 Outcome Measures & Discharge
  • Braden: pressure-injury risk scale (lower = higher risk).
  • Bates-Jensen Wound Assessment Tool (BWAT): scores wound characteristics (size, depth, edges, tunneling, exudate, tissue type) — useful to track healing over time.
  • Pressure mapping / mapping devices identify high-load areas on support surfaces.
  • Goniometry documents joint motion and contracture risk around a wound site.
  • ABI informs arterial involvement and compression decisions.

KEYSafe discharge / transfer criteria

  • Wound healing/progressing (closing, no deepening), patient offloading as taught, and patient/caregiver able to perform or direct the dressing routine.
  • Patient/family can list signs of wound infection and know who to call (red flags education).
  • Plan communicated via documentation and handoff (e.g. SBAR) to the next provider.

💡Pearl

A wound that is NOT progressing despite correct care for 2-4 weeks, or that deepens/widens, triggers re-evaluation: vascular status, nutrition, infection, or decompensation of the underlying disease.

3.10 Wound First-Aid & Miscellaneous Wound Care Facts
  • Fresh wound in the clinic: control bleeding with direct pressure, clean gently with sterile saline, cover with a sterile dressing, and refer for suturing/antibiotic indication per policy.
  • Puncture wounds: higher infection risk (deep contamination); do not seal the puncture.
  • Bite wounds and wounds with retained foreign bodies: physician evaluation, no primary closure unless directed, watch for infection.
  • Wet dressings are changed when saturated; a dressing that stays dry on a highly exudative wound needs a different (more absorbent or NPWT) approach.
  • Prevention of new wounds in at-risk patients: skin inspection daily, footwear that fits, moisture barriers for incontinence, and offloading during all sessions.

⛔Red flag summary

Escalating pain, spreading erythema, new odor, sudden change in exudate, exposed bone/tendon, fever or chills, or a wound that goes deep/black — stop the plan and escalate. These are never 'expected' wound patterns.

KEYHand hygiene & PPE practice

  • Perform hand hygiene before and after every patient, and between dirty and clean tasks (the 'five moments').
  • PPE donning order: gown, then mask/respirator, then goggles/face shield, then gloves. Doffing order (to avoid contamination): gloves, then goggles/face shield, then gown, then mask/respirator — with hand hygiene between steps.

  • Disinfect treatment tables, plinths, and shared equipment between patients; single-use items are never reused.
  • Screen when history suggests rash, cough, fever, or known infection — postpone or protect per policy.

⛔Trap to remember

C. difficile: alcohol hand gel is ineffective against spores — wash with soap and water. This exact nuance appears repeatedly on the exam.

4.2 Body Mechanics & Staff Safety

Safe patient handling protects the patient AND the therapist — staff back injuries are a leading cause of PT work loss. Modern practice replaces manual lifting with equipment whenever possible.

  • Use mechanical lifts (full-sling, stand-assist), ceiling lifts, slider boards, and gait belts for transfers; a single therapist should NOT attempt lifting heavy dependent patients alone.
  • Before any transfer: lock the wheelchair/set up the destination, position the patient's feet, use a gait belt snugly, and coordinate (count of three).
  • Use your legs and wide base of support, keep the load close to your body, avoid twisting, and do not depend on upper-body strength alone.
  • Check equipment before use: wheelchair brakes and armrests, walker/cane tips, plinth straps, and pin locks.
  • Clinical falls: ensure call light within reach, side rails per policy, bed/tilt alarms for high-risk patients, hourly rounding, and a fall-risk flag on the chart.

⚠️Precaution

Never work out of habit — re-check the brakes, re-check the belt, re-check clearance before every single ambulation or transfer.

4.3 Legal Foundations: Licensure, Consent, Documentation

KEYLicensure & scope

  • You practice under the state practice act; scope of practice, supervision requirements, and delegation rules vary by state — PTs should know their own act and endorse/compact privileges context.
  • Practice outside your competence is an ethical and legal violation — refer or seek training.

KEYInformed consent

  • Elements: explanation of the proposed treatment, its risks and benefits, reasonable alternatives, and the right to refuse or withdraw at any time — given to a patient who is competent and volitional.
  • Who consents: the patient if competent; a surrogate/legal guardian for minors or incapacitated adults; emergency treatment may proceed under implied consent.
  • Refusal of treatment does not end your duty — you reassess, explain consequences clearly, document the refusal, and offer alternatives.
  • Advance directives and DNR must be respected during treatment unless a valid in-the-moment order directs otherwise.

4.6 Mandatory Reporting & Duty to Protect
  • Suspected abuse of children, elders, or vulnerable adults MUST be reported to the designated state agency — you do NOT need proof, and you do NOT confront the family first.
  • Report signs: unexplained injuries, contradictory stories, neglect (dehydration, pressure ulcers, untreated medical needs), unusual fear or withdrawal.
  • Impaired or incompetent colleagues suspected of endangering patients should be reported through facility and state channels.
  • Duty to warn (Tarasoff): if a patient poses a serious, imminent threat of harm to a specific identifiable person, you may have a duty to warn that person/authorities — facility policy and state law govern the exact steps.
  • Sexual misconduct and boundary violations are zero-tolerance: no romantic/sexual relationships with current patients, no exploitative gifts, and clear professional boundaries.

⛔Rule

Abuse reporting is a duty, not a choice, and it survives confidentiality. 'I was not sure' is not a valid reason to stay silent when signs are present.

The delegation lines the exam loves

KEYSupervision rules to remember

  • The PT remains responsible for the plan and its outcome even when a PTA delivers treatment — supervision requirements (on-site vs general) vary by state; know the difference and comply.
  • A PTA communicates findings to the PT; any change that would alter the plan triggers a PT re-assessment.
  • An aide/tech can help physically but never performs skilled care independently.
  • Discharge is the PT's call; the PTA reinforces teaching but does not make the discharge decision.

⚠️Trap

The exam distinguishes the aide (non-skilled, supervised) from the PTA (skilled, under PT direction/ supervision) from the PT (evaluator and planner). If a question says 'the assistant should evaluate/decide,' that is the wrong answer.

4.9 Billing, Reimbursement & Fraud Prevention
  • Medicare Part A covers inpatient (hospital, SNF) care; Part B covers outpatient services including PT; therapy must be medically necessary and progress-capable.
  • Documentation must support the billed codes: CPT codes describe the services, modifiers and units matter, and 'incident-to' billing has specific physician-supervision requirements.
  • Fraud and abuse: upcoding (billing a higher service than given), unbundling (billing components separately to inflate payment), fabrication of records — all zero tolerance and grounds for exclusion from Medicare.
  • Medical necessity and skilled care: sessions billed must be performed by an appropriately qualified provider and be necessary — maintenance 'only' programs require specific documentation rules.
  • When you see a billing pattern that looks wrong, report through the facility compliance channel — silence makes you part of it.

⚠️Rule

The patient's payer information is never a reason to change the plan of care; and your notes must match what you actually did and why — that is the backbone of every reimbursement audit.

4.10 Professionalism, Outcomes & the ICF Model

The NPTE increasingly tests functional, patient-centered thinking. The ICF (International Classification of Functioning) frames problems in three layers: body structure/function, activity, and participation — plus personal and environmental factors. Intervention goals are written to function, not just diagnosis.

KEYICF levels in action (know the label for each)

  • Body structure/function: muscle strength 3/5, ROM limitation, pain intensity.
  • Activity: walking distance, stair negotiation, ADL performance.
  • Participation: returning to work, sports, community life.
  • Contextual factors: home stairs, caregiver support, equipment, patient beliefs — they enable or barrier function.

KEYDocumentation & goals

  • Write SMART goals: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound (e.g. 'ambulate 150 feet with a rolling walker, contact-guard assist, within 3 weeks').
  • Document objective, functional measurements (gait speed, 6MWT, TUG, ROM/MMT) at baseline and intervals — outcomes justify the plan.
  • Use outcome measures with known cutoffs (e.g. TUG greater than 13.5 seconds = fall risk; Berg balance below 45/56 = increased risk; gait speed below about 0.8 m/s = falls/mobility concern).
  • Continue competence: CEUs, staying current with evidence-based practice, and honest referral when outside your expertise.

💡Pearl

When an exam question gives you impairment (weakness), activity (can't walk), and participation (can't return to sport), choose the goal written at the FUNCTIONAL level — that is where the NPTE wants your intervention aimed.
